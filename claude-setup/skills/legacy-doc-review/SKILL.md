# Legacy Document Review

## Outcome
A review Dustin can hand to a client and to their attorney that answers three
questions: what these documents say in plain language, where they conflict
with the client's actual accounts and plan, and what to ask the attorney.

The advisor's value here is **coordination** — making sure the financial
picture and the legal documents tell the same story. Not interpretation.

## The hard boundary: this is not legal advice
Dustin is a fiduciary advisor under a broker-dealer/RIA. Interpreting the legal
validity or meaning of estate documents is the practice of law and sits outside
his authority. Crossing that line creates regulatory and liability exposure and
does the client no favors.

- **Never** state that a document is valid, enforceable, properly executed, or
  legally sufficient.
- **Never** advise adding, removing, or changing legal language, or explain
  what a clause "means" as a matter of law.
- **Never** recommend a specific legal structure ("you need an irrevocable
  trust"). You may note that a topic is worth raising with their attorney.
- **Never** give a tax opinion. Route it to the CPA.

If you catch yourself writing a legal conclusion, reframe it as either a
plain-language summary of what the document states, or a question for counsel.

> **Wrong:** "The will is invalid because it isn't properly witnessed."
> **Right:** "Execution requirements are a legal question. **Action: ask the
> estate attorney to confirm the will was executed per state requirements.**"

**In scope:** wills including pour-over wills, revocable and irrevocable
trusts, financial/durable POAs, healthcare POAs and advance directives,
beneficiary designations (retirement accounts, life insurance, annuities,
TOD/POD).

**Out of scope:** drafting or redlining language, tax return prep or formal tax
opinions, anything needing a judgment on validity or enforceability.

## What good looks like

**It catches the expensive disconnects.** This is where the money actually goes
wrong, in rough order of how often and how badly:

- **Beneficiary designations override the will.** Retirement accounts,
  annuities, and life insurance pass by beneficiary form regardless of what the
  will says. A mismatch between the form and the will's apparent intent is the
  single most common and most costly finding in this work.
- **Stale or missing beneficiaries** — blank contingents, an ex-spouse still
  listed, a deceased beneficiary, or "estate" named as beneficiary, which can
  force probate and accelerate income tax on retirement assets.
- **Titling vs. the plan** — accounts the estate plan seems to assume are
  trust-owned but may not be.
- **Trust funding** — an unfunded trust usually doesn't do what the client
  believes it does. Flag that funding needs verifying. Never assume it.
- **SECURE 2.0 exposure** — a trust or non-spouse individual named on a
  retirement account raises the 10-year payout rule and RMD timing (start age
  73 under current law). Flag it for the attorney and CPA. Don't opine on it.
- **Incapacity gaps** — no durable financial POA or no healthcare directive can
  stall the entire financial plan the moment it's needed.
- **Inconsistency and staleness** — conflicting fiduciary appointments across
  documents, or documents predating a marriage, divorce, birth, death, or
  liquidity event.

**It names what's missing.** Gaps are frequently the biggest finding. A
complete legacy set is: will or trust, financial POA, healthcare POA or advance
directive, and current beneficiary designations on every retirement account,
annuity, and policy. If only part of the set arrived, say what's needed to
finish the picture.

**It pulls the roles that matter** from each document, as a neutral summary of
what the document states: executor and successor, guardian nominations,
grantor, trustee and successor, beneficiaries and distribution terms (outright
vs. staged vs. discretionary), whether a POA is durable and whether it reads as
springing or immediate, healthcare agent and successor, per stirpes vs. per
capita, and dates on the face of each document.

**It sorts every finding by owner** — attorney, client, or advisor — each with
a priority.

**It sounds like a guardian, not an alarm.** Calm, protective, plain language.
Even a serious gap is "here's what we want to tighten up," never "here's what's
wrong." Define any unavoidable term in a few words.

## Structure
Open with a TL;DR: what was reviewed, the most important findings, the single
most urgent action. Then: documents reviewed, what's missing, plain-language
summary per document, coordination findings with priorities and why each
matters to the plan, numbered questions for the estate attorney, and an action
list by owner.

## Required disclaimer — include verbatim
> This review was prepared by your financial advisor to coordinate your estate
> documents with your overall financial plan. It is **not legal advice** and is
> not a determination of the validity, enforceability, or legal sufficiency of
> any document. Please consult a licensed estate planning attorney for any
> legal questions and before making changes to your documents. Tax
> considerations should be confirmed with a qualified tax professional.

If LRP has a preferred compliance disclosure block, ask whether to substitute it.
